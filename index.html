01 · Start with a plan

Before you buy

Get the home ready first. The dragon should never be the test run for new equipment.

01

Check it's legal and available

Ask a reputable local store to confirm that bearded dragons can currently be kept in New Zealand and where their animals come from. Rules can change, so check before making plans.

02

Run the setup first

Set up the enclosure and run it for about a week. Record daytime and night temperatures, check every heat source, and fix problems before your dragon arrives.

Before your dragon arrives

Get these essentials sorted

  • Confirm current NZ requirements and availability with a reputable local store.
  • Measure space for an enclosure at least 120 × 60 × 60 cm. See the Reptile One S2M 1266 vivarium.
  • Set up heating, UVB, thermostats, and thermometers, then test them for about a week.
  • Choose a suitable place for feeder insects and find an exotic-pet vet.
  • Agree who can care for the dragon when the family is busy or away.
  • Set household rules for the dog and cats before bringing the dragon home.

Enclosure

At least 120 × 60 × 60 cm (long × deep × tall); bigger is welcome. Wood or PVC holds heat better than glass in a cold room. Choose front-opening doors with locks and a sturdy, level stand. Keep it away from windows, drafts, the floor, and the dog's bed.

Albany option: Reptile One S2M 1266 Vivarium (120 × 60 × 60 cm). Check the size and setup suit your room before ordering.

☼

Heat and temperature

Use a basking lamp at the warm end on a thermostat. A cold Auckland winter room may need a no-light night heat source, also controlled by a thermostat. Check both ends daily and measure the basking surface with a temperature gun.

Shop options: 72W daylight halogen · daylight basking lamps · ceramic night heat. Also see the day/night dimming thermostat and infrared thermometer. Ask the store to match wattage and thermostat type to your enclosure; ceramic heaters need a pulse thermostat.

✦

UVB is essential

Use a UVB tube along the enclosure and run it on a timer for about 10–12 hours daily. Follow the manufacturer's distance and replacement instructions; a tube can stop producing enough UVB before it stops glowing.

Shop options: Arcadia ProT5 39W 14% kit · Reptile One 12% T5 tube · 24-hour light timer. Confirm tube length, fitting, mounting distance, and suitability with the store before buying.

Set up the landscape

Add a stable basking platform, a hide at each end, safe branches or logs, a shallow water dish, and an easy-to-clean feeding spot.

Shop options: large clay cave · large rock cave · reptile habitat accessories. Check hide dimensions and stability for your dragon.

Floor and humidity

For a young dragon, choose a solid, easy-to-clean floor such as tile or paper. Avoid loose substrate that could be swallowed. Keep the enclosure dry; around 30–40% humidity is a general guide.

Keep it safe

Use a thermostat on every heat source. Secure cables, keep lamps clear of anything flammable, and make sure pets cannot open or knock over the enclosure.

03 · Food, water, and good habits

Food and feeding

Age changes the balance. Ask your vet or store to tailor portions and supplements to your dragon.

Growing dragons

More insects, every day

Offer insects 2–3 times a day, plus a small amount of chopped greens daily.

Adult dragons

More greens, every day

Offer fresh leafy greens and vegetables daily, with insects a few times a week.

Feeder insects

Our family rule: no crickets or roaches.

  • Black soldier fly larvae: often called calci-worms; see live soldier worms and ask if they suit as a staple.
  • Silkworms: see the vacuum-sealed Pro Bug silkworms as a packaged option.
  • Superworms: offer only occasionally, as a treat.
  • Mealworms: part of a varied mix, not the main food.

Choose insects no longer than the space between your dragon's eyes. Store them in a lidded tub in a suitable cool place—not the fridge—and follow the supplier's care instructions.

Greens and extras

Staples to ask about: collard, mustard, turnip, and dandelion greens; dandelion flowers; endive; and small amounts of bok choy.

Extras: grated squash, pumpkin, capsicum, cooked sweet potato, and a little fruit as an occasional treat.

Avoid: avocado, rhubarb, wild-caught insects, fireflies, and anything exposed to pesticides. Limit iceberg lettuce, and ask about spinach and kale before feeding them often.

Water and supplements

Provide fresh water in a shallow dish every day. Ask your vet or store which calcium powder (with or without D3) and multivitamin to use, and how often. The right schedule depends on the UVB setup and diet.

Shop options: Reptile One Calcium + D3 · Fluker’s calcium without D3. Choose one only as advised by your vet or store.

A shallow bath is not a replacement for drinking water or veterinary care. Don't force bathing; ask your vet whether it is appropriate for your dragon.

04 · Small routines add up

Daily, weekly, monthly

Ten focused minutes each day can catch little problems before they grow.

Every day10–20 min

Check, feed, refresh

Check both ends and the basking surface temperatures.

Check that lights, heating, and timers are working.

Refresh water and offer the right food for the dragon's age.

Remove droppings and uneaten food.

Look for changes in activity, appetite, eyes, and droppings.

Every week

Clean and keep track

  • Wash and refill the water dish.
  • Wipe surfaces and hides.
  • Give supplements only on the agreed schedule.
  • Weigh your dragon and add it to the care log.
  • Only bathe if advised and appropriate.
Every month

Check the setup

  • Deep-clean with a reptile-safe product; rinse and dry well. See Reptile One Enclosure Sanitiser.
  • Check cables, lamp fittings, and thermostats for wear.
  • Check the UVB tube's replacement date.
Every 6–12 months

Replace and review

  • Replace the UVB tube as its maker recommends.
  • Ask your vet whether a check-up is due.

05 · Calm, careful, and supervised

Handling and family safety

Let your dragon settle in and learn to trust you at its own pace.

1

Give it time

Let your dragon settle for a week or two before handling. Start with short sessions of 5–10 minutes.

2

Support its whole body

Approach slowly from the side, lift gently, and support the body, legs, and tail.

3

Pick a safe moment

Handle only in your bedroom with the door closed. Avoid handling after a meal or when your dragon seems stressed or unwell.

Dog and cat rules

  • Keep the enclosure locked and the bedroom door closed when you're away.
  • Keep the dog and cats away from the enclosure, water, and equipment.
  • Keep the dog out of the room during handling. Never leave the dragon loose or unattended.
  • If a pet fixates on the dragon, block its view and separate them.
Wash your hands after handling your dragon or its equipment. Reptiles can carry Salmonella. Keep their food and cleaning items away from human food, and don't wash enclosure items in the kitchen sink.

06 · Know what to watch for

Health watch

You know your dragon's usual behaviour best. If something feels wrong, call an exotic-pet vet for advice.

Often normal

  • Eating and being active during the day
  • Basking with its mouth open to cool down
  • Shedding in patches as it grows
  • A darker beard from time to time
+

Don't wait and guess

When to call the vet

Get veterinary advice promptly for:

  • Not eating for several days
  • Weakness, wobbling, twitching, or a swollen jaw or legs
  • Sunken eyes, dull skin, or signs of dehydration
  • Runny or very smelly droppings, or no droppings for a long time
  • Swollen, crusty, or closed eyes; tiny moving dots on the skin
  • Sitting unusually still with eyes shut in the cool end

A few useful words

Glossary

Basking
Sitting under heat to warm up; reptiles regulate their body temperature this way.
Gradient
A range from warm to cool inside the enclosure.
UVB
A type of light that helps a dragon make vitamin D3.
Thermostat
A device that controls a heat source to hold a set temperature.
Substrate
The material covering the enclosure floor.
Shedding
When a reptile loses old skin as it grows.
Metabolic bone disease
A serious bone condition that can result from inadequate UVB or calcium.
Impaction
A blockage in the gut, sometimes from swallowing loose substrate.
